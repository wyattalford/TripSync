## Problem Statement

### Domain

The domain is informal social planning among people who already know each other. Friends often want to get dinner, watch a movie, or spend an evening together between classes, work, and other commitments. One person may suggest an activity in a group chat or ask a few friends whether they are around. The group then needs to find a suitable time, decide who is joining, and establish whether the gathering is actually happening.

The primary stakeholders are friends who want to spend time together without making every small gathering require extensive planning. They want opportunities to see each other while retaining flexibility and control over their personal time. A gap between scheduled commitments may be used for homework, rest, or time alone, so an empty calendar does not establish willingness to meet. The relevant information is when someone would welcome an invitation and whether they have accepted a particular plan.

### Stakeholders

- **Person initiating a plan:** Suggests a gathering and wants to find interested friends without repeatedly asking each person when they are available.
- **Invited friend(s):** Decide whether to join and need to understand the proposed time and activity before committing.
- **Friends outside the planning channel:** May be left out when invitations circulate through a particular app or group chat they do not use.

### Bad Situations

The following scenarios illustrate the situations we aim to address.

1. **Friends have overlapping availability, but a tentative plan never becomes a gathering.** On Thursday, John, Walker, Eli, and Noah discuss watching Star Wars on Friday. Everyone expresses interest, but their replies arrive at different times and nobody establishes a start time. On Friday afternoon, John asks, “Are we doing this?” Two friends are still in class, and the conversation stalls. By evening, each assumes the others have become busy spends the evening separately at home. On Saturday, they discover that all four had been willing and available from 7 to 10 PM. A suitable opening existed, but they learned about it after it had passed.

2. **Two friends want company, but neither knows an invitation would be welcome.** Eli finishes an assignment earlier than expected and has two hours before his next commitment. He would like to get dinner with a friend, but his last few invitations reached people who were busy. Rather than message several people again, he eats alone. Noah spends the same period looking for something to do but assumes Eli is still working. When they talk that night, they realize they both were free and would've loved to hang out. Neither had a specific plan to announce, and each person's willingness remained invisible to the other.

3. **An empty calendar creates a misleading expectation of availability.** Walker shares his calendar with John to make arranging gatherings easier. John sees no events on Wednesday evening and suggests watching a movie. Walker had intended to use that time to study and rest, so he declines. John asks which other calendar gaps would work, leaving Walker to explain the purpose of his unscheduled time. After several similar exchanges, Walker stops sharing his calendar. They still need repeated conversations to discover when an invitation would be welcome, and Walker feels that making his schedule visible has made protecting personal time harder.
